# Literature Review: Algorithm Choice for PPO Self-Play in Pokémon Showdown Gen 1 Random Battles

## TL;DR

- **Your convergence worry is theoretically real, not hypothetical: independent/symmetric policy-gradient self-play in a two-player zero-sum game provably cycles or diverges in the last iterate (Bailey & Piliouras 2018; Mertikopoulos et al. 2018), and win-rate-vs-self is blind to it. But it has never been characterized in a game with your exact triple of properties (simultaneous move + hidden info + heavy chance). The single highest-value action is not switching algorithms — it is fixing your instrument (Q4) and building a cross-seed/cross-checkpoint exploitability probe (Q1), because both are cheap, both use artifacts you already have, and neither violates your purity constraint.**
- **Ranked by expected value: (1) paired/common-random-number evaluation across training seeds — the one lever with a proven variance-reduction theorem and the one that unblocks every future comparison; (2) cross-play matrices + an approximate-best-response probe to detect cycling; (3) entropy regularization toward last-iterate convergence (R-NaD/MMD idea, portable as a reward/loss modification without abandoning PPO); (4) an unbiased asymmetric (history+state) critic; then, far behind, (5) full algorithm replacement (NFSP/PSRO/Deep CFR), which is high-cost and mostly demonstrated in alternating-turn games.**
- **The literature does NOT answer your central question — whether pure-self-play PPO converges or cycles specifically in simultaneous-move + hidden-info + heavy-chance games. Every equilibrium-convergence demonstration (DeepNash/Stratego, NFSP/poker, Deep CFR/poker, ESCHER/dark chess) is in an alternating-turn game; every Pokémon RL result in the record uses either human data, no self-play convergence analysis, or search at inference. You are in genuinely uncharacterized territory, which is exactly why the detection instruments matter more than any single algorithm swap.**

## Key Findings

1. **PPO self-play convergence is not guaranteed and the failure is invisible to win-rate.** In two-player zero-sum games, the last iterate of no-regret / gradient dynamics (MWU, FTRL, vanilla policy gradient) is provably non-convergent: Bailey & Piliouras (2018, ACM EC) prove last-iterate divergence of MWU away from interior equilibria; Mertikopoulos, Papadimitriou & Piliouras (2018, SODA) prove FTRL trajectories cycle (Poincaré recurrence) and never converge in the last iterate. Only the *time-average* iterate converges to Nash. Daskalakis, Foster & Golowich (2020, NeurIPS, arXiv:2101.04233) prove independent policy gradient converges in zero-sum Markov games *only* under an asymmetric two-timescale learning-rate rule they show is necessary — symmetric mirror self-play (your setup) has no such guarantee. Mirror-self-play win rate is ~50% by construction whether you are converging or orbiting, so it cannot reveal this.

2. **Regularization is the cheapest theoretically-grounded fix and does not require abandoning PPO.** Perolat et al. (2021, ICML, arXiv:2002.08456) prove that adding a KL-regularization term toward a reference policy converts the recurrent dynamics into ones with a unique convergent fixed point; iterating the reference gives exact Nash convergence (R-NaD). DeepNash (Perolat et al. 2022, *Science*, arXiv:2206.15378) scaled this to Stratego — model-free, no search at inference, learned from scratch by self-play. Its measured results: per DeepLearning.AI's *The Batch*, "winning 97.1 percent of 800 games" against prior Stratego bots; on the Gravon human platform, per the paper, "42 (i.e. 84%)" of ~50 ranked matches were won, yielding "a rating of 1799, which resulted in a 3rd place." Sokota et al. (2023, ICLR, arXiv:2206.05825) magnetic mirror descent (MMD) shows the same entropy-regularization idea yields last-iterate *linear* convergence to a quantal-response (entropy-regularized Nash) equilibrium and is "the first standard reinforcement learning algorithm to achieve empirically competitive results with CFR in tabular settings." **Crucially: DeepNash was demonstrated in Stratego (alternating, hidden, near-deterministic) and MMD in Dark Hex / Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe (alternating, hidden, no chance) — not simultaneous-move-plus-chance games.**

3. **Variance across training seeds can be cut with a proven paired design, and this is your binding constraint.** Sharma (2025, arXiv:2512.24145, "When Does Pairing Seeds Reduce Variance?") formalizes paired-seed evaluation: using identical seeds across the two conditions makes the variance of the difference estimator Var(Δ̂_pair) = Var(Δ̂_ind) − (2/n)·Cov(Y₁,Y₀). When seed-level outcomes are positively correlated (they measured Pearson ρ typically >0.9, often 0.92–0.99, across 22 seeds in a multi-agent economic simulator), the effective sample size gain is ≈ 1/(1−ρ) — a ~10× reduction in runs needed for the same precision is plausible if cross-condition correlation is high. This is an experimental-design change, not an objective change, so it does not touch your purity claim.

4. **Prior Pokémon RL is thin, and none of it validates pure-self-play convergence.** Huang & Lee (2019, IEEE CoG) trained PPO self-play *without* tree search; per the paper, "At the end of 500 training iterations, RL-rb attains a 1677 Glicko-1 rating, which roughly corresponds to a 72% chance of defeating an opponent selected uniformly at random from the ladder" (Gen7 Random Battle). Wang (2024, MIT MEng thesis, dspace 1721.1/153888) augmented PPO with MCTS at test-time and "achieve a 1756 Glicko-1 and 79.5% GXE on the Gen4RandomBattle ladder." Simões et al. (2020, ICARSC) built a Pokémon battle environment and used equilibrium-seeking algorithms (WPL, GIGA) on a reduced problem. The strongest agents overall use search + human priors — PokéChamp (Karten et al. 2025, ICML, arXiv:2503.04094): "When powered by GPT-4o, it achieves a win rate of 76% against the best existing LLM-based bot and 84% against the strongest rule-based bot… PokéChamp attains a projected Elo of 1300-1500" (Gen 9 OU) — or human data (PokéLLMon, Hu et al. 2024: 49% ladder win rate). **No published Pokémon agent demonstrates pure-self-play equilibrium convergence or measures its own exploitability.**

5. **Simultaneous-move nodes require mixed strategies; naive maximin/expectimax is exploitable.** Lanctot, Lisý & Winands (2013) and the surrounding SM-MCTS literature show that on simultaneous-move nodes, deterministic selection (Decoupled UCT) can produce more exploitable strategies than regret-matching / Exp3, which converge toward the Nash mixed strategy of the stage game; Shafiei, Sturtevant & Schaeffer showed UCT does not converge to Nash in simultaneous games and can be exploited. Your one-ply expectimax picks a deterministic best response to an assumed opponent distribution and is therefore exploitable at any node where the stage game has no pure equilibrium (common in Pokémon: stay-vs-switch mind-games).

## Details

### Q1 — Convergence & detection (HIGH VALUE; partially answered by theory, unanswered for your game class)

**The theory.** Your game is two-player zero-sum, which is the good case: a Nash equilibrium exists and is exchangeable, so "the" equilibrium is a well-defined target. The problem is the *dynamics*, not existence. In order of relevance:

- **Bailey & Piliouras (2018, ACM EC), "Multiplicative Weights Update in Zero-Sum Games":** discrete-time MWU/FTRL exhibits last-iterate divergence — for games with interior equilibria they prove a nonnegative lower bound on movement *away* from equilibrium. The time-average still converges (Freund–Schapire no-regret); the last iterate does not.
- **Mertikopoulos, Papadimitriou & Piliouras (2018, SODA), "Cycles in Adversarial Regularized Learning":** continuous-time FTRL (includes replicator/MWU) forms periodic orbits around interior equilibria — Poincaré recurrence — and does not converge. Nuance: these are cycles, not asymptotically stable limit cycles (perturbing initialization gives a different orbit).
- **Daskalakis, Foster & Golowich (2020, NeurIPS, arXiv:2101.04233), "Independent Policy Gradient Methods for Competitive RL":** independent PG in two-player zero-sum Markov games converges to min-max equilibrium *only* under a two-timescale (asymmetric) learning-rate rule, which they prove is necessary. Symmetric mirror self-play does not satisfy this. (Note: this paper is by Daskalakis, Foster & Golowich — not the Ilyas/Syrgkanis/Zeng author set, which authored a separate optimism paper.)
- **Mazumdar & Ratliff (2018) / Mazumdar, Ratliff & Sastry (SIAM J. Math. Data Science):** gradient-based learning in continuous games can have stable non-Nash attractors and limit cycles due to the non-symmetric game Jacobian.

Practical translation: **vanilla independent PPO mirror self-play can orbit or drift among mutually-exploitable policies while win-rate-vs-current-self stays pinned at ~50%.** This is proven for zero-sum gradient dynamics in general. What is *not* established is how strongly it bites in a heavy-chance, hidden-info, simultaneous-move game with ~30-turn episodes and terminal-only reward — the chance and terminal reward inject large gradient noise that may dominate or mask cycling. No paper measures this for your class.

**Non-transitivity context.** Balduzzi et al. (2019, "Open-ended Learning in Symmetric Zero-sum Games") and Czarnecki et al. (2020, NeurIPS, "Real World Games Look Like Spinning Tops") argue real games have a strong transitive axis plus a cyclic ("spinning top") dimension widest at intermediate skill and narrowing near the top. Consequence: a *single* self-play lineage can get stuck rotating in the cyclic band; a population is the standard remedy. Empirically validated on nine real two-player zero-sum games — but not on a simultaneous-move-plus-chance game.

**Detection using only artifacts you already have** (the actionable part):

1. **Cross-play matrix between seeds and checkpoints.** Round-robin head-to-head among checkpoints c₁…c_k (across your 3 seeds), N games per cell with paired RNG (Q4). Cycling shows up as **intransitivity** (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A, off-diagonals significantly ≠ 50%). Convergence shows up as the matrix collapsing toward 50% for late checkpoints. This is the "minimum win-rate against all past versions" forgetting metric AlphaStar reported (Vinyals et al. 2019, *Nature*).
2. **Monotonicity of "beats its own past."** Late-checkpoint win rate vs each ancestor. Healthy transitive progress: monotone ≥50% against all ancestors. Cycling/forgetting: late model *loses* to some middle-aged ancestor.
3. **Approximate-best-response (exploitability) probe.** Timbers et al. (2022, IJCAI, "Approximate Exploitability: Learning a Best Response," arXiv:2004.09677): freeze your policy, train a fresh RL best-responder; the BR's win rate is a *lower bound* on your exploitability. They report "BR managed to exploit Slumbot by 1259 mbb/h when ABR acted using the FCPA betting abstraction," and showed AlphaZero Go agents are substantially exploitable by a learned BR (up to ~90% with a simulation curriculum). This is the most direct test of "near equilibrium vs merely near a fixed point of self-play." It costs one extra training run per probed checkpoint, and — importantly — a best-response *probe* is an evaluation, not a training opponent, so it respects your purity constraint. (Caveat: ISMCTS-BR as published uses a game model for its search; the model-free variant is simply "freeze policy, train PPO best-responder.")
4. **Nash averaging / α-rank** (Balduzzi et al. 2018 "Re-evaluating Evaluation"; Omidshafiei et al. 2019 "α-Rank") on the cross-play matrix gives an evaluation invariant to redundant/cyclic agents and surfaces non-transitivity that raw Elo hides.

### Q4 — Variance: the instrument (HIGHEST VALUE; best-answered of your five questions)

**Paired evaluation / common random numbers ACROSS TRAINING SEEDS — this exists and there is a theorem.** Sharma (2025, arXiv:2512.24145) is the direct hit: it treats the *seed* as the unit of randomization, pairs it across the two conditions (so baseline and intervention see the same init, same env chance draws, same opponent schedule), and estimates the effect as a within-seed difference. **Theorem 1:** Var(Δ̂_pair) = Var(Δ̂_ind) − (2/n)Cov(Y₁,Y₀); with matched variances, effective sample size r_eff ≈ r/(1−ρ). They measured seed-level ρ typically >0.9 across 22 seeds. **This maps exactly onto your problem:** run the control and intervention recipes with *identical* seed streams for init, env, and opponent sampling, differing only in the intervention, and compare paired differences. At ρ=0.7 effective sample size roughly triples; at ρ=0.9 it's ~10×. That can move your detection threshold from ~0.10 win-rate toward the 0.02–0.05 range without more seeds.

**The caveat the theorem exposes:** variance reduction is proportional to ρ, the *cross-condition* seed-level correlation. Interventions that reroute the whole training trajectory (large entropy-coefficient or encoder changes) may *decorrelate* the paired runs and shrink the benefit. Pairing is strongest for small architectural/hyperparameter deltas. Measure the paired ρ per intervention; if it's near zero, pairing bought nothing.

**Two RNG levels, different behavior:**
- *Evaluation-episode CRN* (pair the eval battles' chance draws between two fixed policies): unambiguously helps; standard variance reduction. Patterson et al. ("Empirical Design in RL") explicitly recommend paired statistical comparisons at the analysis stage. Cheap — do it now.
- *Training-seed CRN* (pair init + env + opponent schedule across whole runs): the Sharma result — higher-leverage but more fragile, because a design change can decorrelate trajectories.

**Statistics for 3–10 seed comparisons — what applies to a single-task terminal win rate and what doesn't:**
- **Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer (2018, "How Many Random Seeds?"; 2019, "A Hitchhiker's Guide to Statistical Comparisons of RL Algorithms," arXiv:1904.06979):** directly applicable — a single-metric, two-algorithm power analysis, exactly your case. Use their machinery (Welch's t-test, bootstrap CIs, empirical false-positive/power curves vs effect size and n) to size seed counts. With sd~0.06 and an *unpaired* Welch test, detecting a 0.03 difference at 80% power/α=0.05 needs on the order of ~60+ seeds per arm — infeasible. This is *why* pairing (which attacks the variance term) is the only realistic route, and why more eval games per seed alone won't save you: they cut within-seed noise but not the cross-seed init/opponent-trajectory variance that dominates.
- **Henderson et al. (2018, "Deep RL That Matters," arXiv:1709.06560):** applies as a cautionary result — two sets of 5 seeds of the *same* algorithm looked statistically different purely from seed variance (TRPO HalfCheetah, "t = -9.0916, p = 0.0016"). This is precisely your sd~0.06 problem and the canonical citation that the instrument, not the idea, is at fault.
- **Agarwal et al. (2021, NeurIPS, "Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" / rliable, arXiv:2108.13264):** *partially* applies. Its core machinery — interquartile mean, performance profiles, stratified bootstrap CIs — is designed for *aggregating across a suite of tasks*. You have a single task, so the aggregation-across-tasks argument does not transfer. What *does* transfer: report interval estimates (bootstrap CIs) not point estimates, and treat per-seed final win rates as your sample for an IQM. Don't oversell rliable as solving a single-task comparison; it was built for the multi-task regime.
- **AdaStop (Mathieu et al. 2023, arXiv:2306.10882):** sequential statistical testing purpose-built for deep RL agent comparison — stop running seeds once significance is reached (or not). Directly applicable; adopt it as your "sequential testing" answer.
- **Jordan et al. (2020, "Evaluating the Performance of RL Algorithms"):** relevant for a rigorous, hyperparameter-inclusive protocol; more useful when comparing *algorithms* than for within-recipe A/Bs.

**Control variates / antithetic sampling.** Control variates are already inside PPO (the value baseline). For *evaluation*, a per-episode control variate (predicted-outcome-minus-realized, or a damage-differential proxy correlated with the win) reduces win-rate estimator variance. Arnold et al. (2022, AISTATS) demonstrated randomized quasi-Monte Carlo yields lower gradient-estimator variance than plain Monte Carlo (95% CIs over 30 seeds) — an existence proof that QMC/antithetic ideas port to RL, though not to terminal win-rate estimation specifically. Antithetic sampling (pairing a chance draw with its mirror) is hard to define cleanly in a branching battle simulator; I found no RL work demonstrating it for terminal win-rate variance. Treat as unproven for your case.

**Seed-variance decomposition (init vs env stochasticity vs opponent trajectory).** No published work cleanly decomposes cross-seed win-rate variance into these components for a self-play RL agent. The paired-seed design *is* the practical tool: pair init only (vary env+opponent), then env only, etc., and read off which pairing collapses the variance. Small experiment, and a genuinely novel measurement for your write-up. Report it as unmeasured in the literature.

### Q2 — Alternatives, ranked by evidence and porting cost

**Property key (name the game every time):**
- Poker (Leduc, Flop/Limit/No-limit Hold'em): **alternating** turns, hidden info, chance. NOT simultaneous.
- Stratego: **alternating** turns, hidden info, near-deterministic combat. NOT simultaneous, NOT high-chance.
- Dark Hex / Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe / Dark Chess: **alternating**, hidden info, no chance.
- StarCraft II: real-time (effectively simultaneous), hidden info, some chance. Closest on structure — but used human data.
- Goofspiel / Tron / Oshi-Zumo: **simultaneous** move, little hidden info, low/no chance. Closest on the simultaneous-move axis.
- Pokémon: **simultaneous** move, hidden info, **high chance**. Your game — essentially unique in having all three at once.

**1. Entropy/reward regularization (R-NaD / DeepNash; MMD).** Demonstrated: Stratego (alternating, hidden, low-chance) for DeepNash; Dark Hex & Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe (alternating, hidden, no chance) for MMD. Beat: DeepNash beat prior Stratego bots (97.1% of 800 games) and reached 3rd/top-3 human on Gravon (84% of ~50 ranked matches, rating 1799). Search at inference: **no** — the key attraction. Porting cost onto your PPO: **LOW-to-MODERATE** — a loss/reward modification (KL penalty toward a slowly-updated reference policy), not a new architecture. Most PPO-compatible equilibrium method. Original compute: DeepNash used large-scale distributed training; the *idea* scales down (MMD ran on small games). **Best "if I must change the objective" option, and it arguably preserves purity** (still pure self-play from random init; you only regularize the dynamics). Caveat: never demonstrated with heavy chance + simultaneous moves.

**2. PSRO / league training (Lanctot et al. 2017, arXiv:1711.00832; AlphaStar, Vinyals et al. 2019).** Demonstrated: Leduc poker, first-person-shooter, StarCraft II. Beat: AlphaStar reached Grandmaster / top-0.2% in StarCraft II (real-time, hidden, some chance) — the closest-to-your-properties success — **but used human replays for initialization and KL-anchoring**, violating purity. PSRO's core finding: independent RL (InRL) *overfits* to opponents (they introduce joint-policy correlation to quantify it) and a population meta-game fixes it. Search at inference: no (network policy), but you maintain a *population* and a Nash meta-solver over the empirical payoff matrix. Porting cost: **HIGH** — population manager + meta-solver + k× compute. On CPU-only single-dev this is heavy. Value: directly addresses the cycling/non-transitivity risk. Compromise: a *minimal* league (small archive of past checkpoints, sample opponents from it) captures much of the anti-cycling benefit cheaply and uses no human data.

**3. Neural Fictitious Self-Play (Heinrich & Silver 2016, arXiv:1603.01121).** Demonstrated: Leduc poker (converged to approx-Nash where DQN diverged) and Limit Texas Hold'em (alternating, hidden, chance) — approached prior state-of-the-art/superhuman. Search at inference: no. Porting cost: **MODERATE-to-HIGH** — two networks (best-response RL net + average-strategy supervised net over a reservoir buffer of your historical actions) with anticipatory-dynamics mixing. It is the classic "average-iterate converges" instantiation — the theoretical fix for Q1 cycling. Never shown in a simultaneous-move or high-chance game. You'd deploy the *average*-policy network, not the RL net.

**4. Deep CFR / DREAM / ESCHER.** Deep CFR (Brown, Lerer, Gross, Sandholm 2019, ICML, arXiv:1811.00164) — Flop/Limit Hold'em (alternating, hidden, chance); "Deep CFR reaches an exploitability of 37 mbb/g while NFSP converges to 47 mbb/g" in FHP (5 replicates); **requires game-tree traversal** — poor fit for a black-box simulator. DREAM (Steinberger, Lerer & Brown 2020, arXiv:2006.10410) — Leduc/Flop Hold'em, **model-free** ("does not require access to a perfect simulator"), beat model-free baselines, reported as mean±std over 3 runs; most relevant of the three for you. ESCHER (McAleer et al. 2023, ICLR, arXiv:2206.04122) — Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe/Dark Hex/Dark Chess (alternating, hidden, no chance), **model-free, no importance sampling**, "beat DREAM and NFSP in a head-to-head competition over 90% of the time" in dark chess (exact game count not stated). Search at inference: no for all three. Porting cost: **VERY HIGH** — a different algorithmic family (regret minimization over information sets), not a PPO modification; adopting one means rewriting the learner. **None demonstrated in a simultaneous-move or heavy-chance game.** Not recommended given your constraints.

**5. Player of Games / ReBeL (search-based).** Demonstrated: poker, Scotland Yard, chess, Go — all alternating. Require search at inference and a model. Prohibitive for your setup, and search-at-inference is something you've deliberately kept minimal. Skip.

**The distinction you asked for — "demonstrated in your game class" vs "assumed to transfer":** *Nothing* here was demonstrated in a game with simultaneous moves AND hidden info AND heavy chance. DeepNash/R-NaD is hidden-info-but-alternating-low-chance (Stratego). NFSP/Deep CFR/DREAM/ESCHER are alternating poker/dark-games. AlphaStar is closest on properties (real-time≈simultaneous, hidden, some chance) but used human data. The simultaneous-move-specific work (Goofspiel/Tron) has little hidden info and little chance. **Every recommendation to adopt one of these for Pokémon is an assumed transfer, not a demonstrated one — flag this in any claim you make.**

### Q3 — Hidden state

**Asymmetric/privileged critic (critic sees opponent's team, actor doesn't).** The intuitive version is *unsound*. Baisero & Amato (2022, AAMAS, "Unbiased Asymmetric RL under Partial Observability," arXiv:2105.11674) show the common state-only asymmetric critic (à la Pinto et al. 2017) breaks the policy-gradient theorem and can be arbitrarily biased; their fix conditions the critic on the **⟨history, state⟩ tuple** (not state alone), exploiting privileged info while staying unbiased. Lyu et al. (2022, AAAI, "A Deeper Understanding of State-Based Critics," arXiv:2201.01221; 2023 JAIR) prove state-based critics introduce bias in the policy gradient and show empirically that centralizing the critic is **not strictly beneficial and can hurt**; a survey summarizes: state-based critics "are unsound in partially observable environments… incorrect and could be arbitrarily bad," working well only in near-fully-observable domains (e.g., SMAC). **The clear finding is a negative one — do not feed the opponent's hidden team to a state-only critic. If you want privileged info, condition the critic on ⟨your observation-history, opponent team⟩ jointly.** Moderate cost (critic input only; actor untouched) and theoretically the right way.

**Belief modeling over hidden features (opponent's set/item/HP).** In Hanabi (cooperative, not zero-sum) belief modeling helps, but that's a different objective. For competitive hidden-info, explicit belief auxiliary losses (predict opponent's revealed-later attributes from history) are a reasonable auxiliary task, but I found no clean effect-size number in a zero-sum simultaneous-move game. Plausible-but-unmeasured for your class.

**Recurrence (GRU/LSTM vs frame-stacking).** Ni, Eysenbach & Salakhutdinov (2022, ICML, "Recurrent Model-Free RL Can Be a Strong Baseline for Many POMDPs," arXiv:2110.05038) is the key result: "We compare to 21 environments from 6 prior specialized methods and find that our implementation achieves greater sample efficiency and asymptotic performance than these methods on 18/21 environments." Translation: recurrence is worth it for a POMDP like yours (progressive team revelation is a memory problem), and a plain GRU over battle history is a strong, low-risk baseline. Their finding also warns recurrence is *finicky* (architecture/hparams matter more than the memory mechanism). Moderate porting cost (DeepSets-per-turn → DeepSets-then-GRU-over-turns).

### Q5 — Simultaneous-move search

**Naive maximin/expectimax at a simultaneous node is systematically exploitable.** In a simultaneous-move stage game whose equilibrium is mixed (generic — stay-vs-switch), any deterministic response is a pure strategy a knowledgeable opponent can exploit. The SM-MCTS literature confirms this empirically:
- Lanctot, Lisý & Winands (2013, "MCTS in Simultaneous Move Games with Applications to Goofspiel") and the Tron study: **regret-matching and Online Outcome Sampling produce less exploitable strategies than UCT/Decoupled-UCT**; all variants were less exploitable than UCT, and in Goofspiel regret-matching/OOS were best. Shafiei, Sturtevant & Schaeffer: UCT does not converge to Nash in simultaneous games and *can be exploited*.
- Bošanský et al. (2016, "Algorithms for Computing Strategies in Two-Player Simultaneous Move Games") is the survey; the correct per-node solution is to solve the stage game for a **mixed** strategy (LP / regret-matching / CFR at each node), not to take a maximin/expectimax point value.

**So the literature says a mixed-strategy solve at each simultaneous node is required to avoid exploitability.** Your one-ply expectimax is exploitable at any mixed-equilibrium node. Low-cost fix faithful to your setup: at the current node, build the small stage-game payoff matrix (~9 of your actions × opponent's estimated actions, values from your value net / one-ply rollout), solve with a small matrix-game LP or a few regret-matching iterations, and **sample** from the resulting mixed strategy rather than argmax. Tiny matrices, cheap, and it directly reduces exploitability.

**Depth-2 vs depth-1 gains.** Pokémon-specific evidence: Wang (2024, MIT) adding MCTS lookahead on a PPO value net moved from Huang & Lee's search-free 1677 Glicko / 72% GXE to 1756 Glicko / 79.5% GXE — but different generations (Gen7 vs Gen4), so *not* a clean controlled A/B (flag it). The prior strongest gen7 randbats agent used two-turn lookahead + a handcrafted heuristic and peaked around rank 33 / Glicko ~1677. PokéChamp (Karten et al. 2025) uses minimax depth with LLM-estimated values (76%/84% vs bot baselines). I found **no clean, controlled depth-1→depth-2 win-rate delta in a simultaneous-move-plus-chance game**; the general SM-MCTS papers measure exploitability reduction, not a single depth-2 win-rate number. Report depth-2 gain as directionally positive but not cleanly quantified for your class.

## Recommendations (prioritized by expected value given your constraints)

**Tier 1 — Fix the instrument (do first; cheapest, unblock everything, purity-safe):**

1. **Adopt paired/CRN evaluation now.** For any A/B, run control and intervention with identical seed streams (init, env chance, opponent schedule) and compare within-seed differences (Sharma 2025). Measure and report the paired ρ per intervention. Expected effect: effective-sample-size gain ~3× (ρ=0.7) to ~10× (ρ=0.9) — what moves you from a 0.10 floor toward 0.02–0.05. **Trigger to change course:** if a given intervention's paired ρ ≈ 0, pairing bought nothing (it decorrelates trajectories) — fall back to more seeds, sized by Colas power analysis.
2. **Add evaluation-episode CRN + a per-episode control variate** (predicted-outcome baseline). Free variance reduction on the eval side; do it regardless of 1.
3. **Adopt AdaStop (sequential testing)** to stop spending seeds the moment a comparison resolves (or is hopeless).
4. **Report bootstrap CIs / IQM over per-seed win rates** (rliable machinery, single-task caveat noted). Stop reporting bare point estimates.

**Tier 2 — Build the detection probe (answers Q1 with artifacts you have):**

5. **Cross-play matrix across seeds and checkpoints** with paired RNG; compute Elo/Bradley-Terry with CIs *and* check for intransitivity and the "loses to its own ancestor" forgetting signal. **Trigger:** if late checkpoints are intransitive or lose to mid-training ancestors, you have empirical cycling — add regularization (Tier 3) or an opponent archive.
6. **Approximate-best-response exploitability probe** (Timbers et al. 2022): periodically freeze the policy and train a from-scratch RL best-responder; its win rate lower-bounds your exploitability. **Trigger:** if a from-scratch BR reaches >~65–70% against your "converged" agent, your self-play fixed point is far from equilibrium and an equilibrium method (Tier 3) is justified. It's an evaluation, not a training opponent — respects purity.

**Tier 3 — Only if Tier 2 shows real cycling/exploitability, change the dynamics (not the paradigm):**

7. **Entropy/KL regularization toward a slowly-updated reference policy (R-NaD/MMD idea).** Lowest-cost equilibrium method, expressible as a loss modification to your existing PPO, no search at inference, and it plausibly preserves "pure self-play from random init." First thing to try if cycling is confirmed.
8. **Minimal opponent archive ("FSP-lite"):** sample training opponents from a small pool of your own past checkpoints instead of only current-self. Cheap anti-cycling insurance, no human data, no population meta-solver. A half-step toward PSRO/league without the compute.

**Tier 4 — Hidden state & search (independent; moderate value):**

9. **Recurrence:** add a GRU over turn history (Ni et al. 2022). Low-risk; addresses progressive revelation.
10. **Unbiased asymmetric critic:** if using privileged info, condition the critic on ⟨history, opponent-team⟩ jointly, never state alone (Baisero & Amato 2022; Lyu et al. 2022).
11. **Mixed-strategy stage-game solve at inference nodes** instead of expectimax argmax: build the small per-node payoff matrix, solve with LP/regret-matching, then sample. Directly reduces the exploitability your current one-ply expectimax carries.

**Do NOT (given your constraints):** adopt Deep CFR/DREAM/ESCHER (different algorithmic family, model/traversal assumptions, none demonstrated in your game class, very high porting cost); adopt full PSRO/AlphaStar league (compute-prohibitive on CPU single-dev; AlphaStar leaned on human data); or add heavy search at inference before fixing the mixed-strategy exploitability of the search you already run.

## Caveats & what the literature does NOT answer

- **Q1 (convergence for your game class): NOT answered.** The cycling/last-iterate theory (Bailey–Piliouras, Mertikopoulos et al., Daskalakis–Foster–Golowich) is proven for zero-sum gradient dynamics in general, but its *magnitude* under heavy chance + simultaneous moves + terminal-only reward is unmeasured. Whether chance noise masks or amplifies cycling in Pokémon is unknown.
- **Q2 (alternatives in your game class): NOT answered.** No equilibrium method — R-NaD, NFSP, PSRO, Deep CFR, DREAM, ESCHER — has been demonstrated in a game with simultaneous moves AND hidden info AND heavy chance. Every recommendation is an assumed transfer. Stratego (DeepNash) is alternating and near-deterministic; poker (NFSP/CFR) is alternating; AlphaStar is closest but used human data.
- **Q3 (hidden state effect sizes): PARTIALLY answered.** The *negative* result is solid (state-only critics can hurt; use ⟨history,state⟩). Positive effect sizes for belief modeling in zero-sum simultaneous-move games are unmeasured. Recurrence is well-supported in POMDPs generally (Ni et al.) but not specifically for your game.
- **Q4 (variance): BEST answered.** Paired-seed theory (Sharma 2025), power analysis (Colas et al.), and cautionary seed-variance results (Henderson et al.) all apply. Gaps: no published decomposition of cross-seed win-rate variance into init vs env vs opponent-trajectory for self-play RL (you'd measure it — and it'd be novel); antithetic sampling for terminal win-rate is unproven; rliable's aggregation is multi-task and only partially transfers to your single task.
- **Q5 (simultaneous-move search): answered qualitatively, not quantitatively for your class.** That naive maximin/expectimax is exploitable and mixed-strategy node solving is required is well-established (Lanctot et al., Bošanský et al.). But a clean controlled depth-1→depth-2 win-rate gain in a simultaneous-move-plus-chance game does not exist in the record; the Pokémon search results (Wang 2024; PokéChamp) are confounded by generation, human priors, or LLM value functions.
- **Sample-size honesty:** several key numbers rest on small or non-controlled setups — DeepNash's Gravon ranking is live-ladder play (~50 matches, not a fixed-N controlled experiment), and its 97.1%/800-games figure is vs bots; Pokémon ladder GXE/Glicko figures (Huang & Lee 72%; Wang 79.5%) are ladder outcomes, not seeded controlled runs; DREAM reports 3-run and ESCHER's >90% head-to-head lacks a stated game count; Deep CFR's 37-vs-47 mbb/g is over 5 replicates. Treat all cross-paper win-rate comparisons as indicative, not decisive — which is, again, the whole reason to fix your own instrument before trusting anyone's numbers, including your own.
