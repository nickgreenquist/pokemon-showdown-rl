# Literature Review: Is Independent PPO in Pure Mirror Self-Play the Right Algorithm for Gen 1 Pokémon Showdown Random Battles?

## TL;DR
- **No convergence guarantee exists for your exact recipe, and the failure it invites is invisible to win rate.** Independent/self-play policy gradient in a two-player zero-sum *simultaneous-move imperfect-information stochastic* game with function approximation is not covered by any positive convergence theorem; the relevant theory (Mertikopoulos–Papadimitriou–Piliouras 2018; Daskalakis–Foster–Golowich 2020; Wei et al. 2021) predicts cycling/recurrence unless you add regularization or two-timescale structure your recipe lacks. Your fixed entropy bonus does *not* by itself buy last-iterate convergence to the quantal-response equilibrium — that requires a KL-to-reference (proximal) term as in Magnetic Mirror Descent / R-NaD, not an entropy bonus.
- **Your instrument is the binding constraint, and it is fixable without touching hyperparameters.** A ~0.10 minimum detectable effect is dominated by *training-seed* variance, not evaluation binomial noise; more eval games alone will not help. The high-leverage moves are (a) cross-seed and within-run checkpoint round-robins to measure intransitivity directly, (b) approximate-exploitability best responses using your own PPO code, and (c) common-random-number / seed-locked paired evaluation exploiting Showdown's explicit PRNG seed. These detect cycling that win rate hides.
- **Keep PPO, but add one thing first: an MMD-style KL-to-a-periodically-refreshed-reference term (a few lines), plus a past-checkpoint opponent pool.** This is the lowest-cost, best-evidenced change consistent with your purity constraint. Do not switch to Deep CFR/NFSP/R-NaD as a first move — the evidence that they help *specifically in a heavy-chance simultaneous-move game* is weak-to-none, and their integration cost is large. The literature has **no** demonstration that pure self-play from scratch reaches a strong level in *any* Pokémon Showdown format; every strong Showdown agent used human data, search, or both.

## 1. Convergence theory and what it actually implies for your recipe

**Framing.** Gen 1 random battles is a two-player zero-sum game that is simultaneous-move, imperfect-information, and heavily stochastic. Formally it is a zero-sum *partially observed stochastic game* / imperfect-information extensive-form game (IIEFG) with chance. This matters because the convergence literature is sharply stratified by exactly these axes, and the positive results live almost entirely in strictly easier classes.

**1.1 The baseline negative result: gradient/replicator dynamics cycle.** Mertikopoulos, Papadimitriou & Piliouras, "Cycles in Adversarial Regularized Learning" (SODA 2018, arXiv:1709.02738) prove that when both players run *regularized* no-regret learning (follow-the-regularized-leader / mirror descent, of which multiplicative weights and softmax policy gradient are instances) in a zero-sum game, the continuous-time dynamics are **Poincaré recurrent**: "almost every trajectory revisits any (arbitrarily small) neighborhood of its starting point infinitely often." The iterates cycle around the equilibrium rather than converging to it, and this is robust to the choice of regularizer and to affine payoff transforms. Bailey & Piliouras (2018) show the discrete-time analogue (multiplicative weights) spirals *outward*. The classical fact that fictitious play and no-regret dynamics converge to Nash in zero-sum games is a statement about the **time-average** of play (the empirical distribution), *not* the **last iterate**. Your deployed policy is the last iterate. So the folk belief "self-play in a zero-sum game converges to Nash" is true only for the time-average, which you are not keeping.

**1.2 What a fixed entropy bonus does and does not buy.** There is a precise and important distinction here. A *fixed entropy bonus* added to the PPO objective changes which game you are solving: it defines a regularized game whose solution is a **quantal response equilibrium** (QRE) / entropy-regularized Nash equilibrium (McKelvey–Palfrey; Mertikopoulos & Sandholm, "Learning in Games via Reinforcement and Regularization," Math. of OR 2016). But adding the entropy term to the *objective* is not the same as getting *last-iterate convergence* to that QRE. Convergence to the QRE requires the update to take a **proximal / mirror-descent step against a reference policy** — i.e., a KL(π‖π_ref) term where π_ref is periodically refreshed toward the current policy. This is exactly the content of:
- **Magnetic Mirror Descent (MMD)**, Sokota, D'Orazio, Kolter, Loizou, Lanctot, Mitliagkas, Brown, Kroer, "A Unified Approach to Reinforcement Learning, Quantal Response Equilibria, and Two-Player Zero-Sum Games" (ICLR 2023, arXiv:2206.05825). MMD = mirror descent + a magnetic/proximal term pulling toward a slowly-updated reference. They "prove that MMD converges linearly to quantal response equilibria (i.e., entropy regularized Nash equilibria) in extensive-form games — this is the first time linear convergence has been proven for a first order solver," and they show MMD "can produce results competitive with those of PPO" on single-agent Atari/MuJoCo, explicitly positioning the update as PPO-like. The key point for you: the *entropy bonus you already have is not the MMD term*; MMD's convergence comes from the KL-to-reference proximal term, which your recipe does not have.
- **Regularized Nash Dynamics (R-NaD)**, Perolat et al., "From Poincaré Recurrence to Convergence in Imperfect Information Games: Finding Equilibrium via Regularization" (ICML 2021), scaled to **DeepNash** (Perolat et al., Science 2022, arXiv:2206.15378). The title is literally the arc of this section: regularization toward a reference policy "converges to an approximate Nash equilibrium, instead of 'cycling' around it, by directly modifying the underlying multi-agent learning dynamics."

**Implication:** your recipe (clipped PPO surrogate + *fixed* entropy bonus + shared network for both sides) sits in the regime the 2018 recurrence result describes, not the regime the MMD/R-NaD results describe. The shared network for both players makes this a symmetric single-population dynamic, which is the exact setting of the replicator/FoReL recurrence analyses. You have no theoretical reason to expect last-iterate convergence, and a specific theoretical reason (Poincaré recurrence) to expect cycling.

**1.3 Markov-game results, and why they don't rescue you.** There is a set of positive results for zero-sum *stochastic (Markov) games*, but read the fine print:
- Daskalakis, Foster & Golowich, "Independent Policy Gradient Methods for Competitive Reinforcement Learning" (NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2101.04233): independent policy gradient converges to min-max equilibrium "as long as their learning rates follow a two-timescale rule (which is necessary)" — one player must learn faster. Symmetric same-rate updates — what you run with a shared network — are exactly what this result excludes. It also assumes tabular/full-observation stochastic games, not imperfect information or function approximation.
- Wei, Lee, Zhang & Luo, "Last-Iterate Convergence of Decentralized Optimistic Gradient Descent/Ascent in Infinite-Horizon Competitive Markov Games" (COLT 2021, arXiv:2102.04540): last-iterate convergence, but requires the **optimistic/extragradient** gradient variant (not vanilla PG), and again perfect information / tabular.
- Cen, Chi et al. ("Faster Last-Iterate Convergence of Policy Optimization in Zero-Sum Markov Games," arXiv:2210.01050) and related entropy-regularized extragradient work: faster last-iterate rates, but again optimistic updates + entropy regularization + tabular Markov games.

Every one of these needs at least one of {two-timescale, optimism/extragradient, tabular, perfect information}. **None covers imperfect-information extensive-form games with function approximation.** Be explicit: for your problem class — IIEFG + chance + neural function approximation — there is no known convergence guarantee for any policy-gradient method, including the regularized ones. R-NaD and MMD have last-iterate *proofs* only at the tabular/first-order-feedback level; DeepNash is empirical evidence that the recipe scales, not a proof.

**1.4 Empirical evidence that naive self-play cycles and overfits.**
- Lanctot et al., "A Unified Game-Theoretic Approach to Multiagent RL" (NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1711.00832): defines **Joint Policy Correlation (JPC)**. Independent RL self-play produces policies that overfit their co-training partner; when you take independently-trained policies (different seeds) and cross-play them, performance drops sharply — their metric "makes clear that independent RL does very poorly at generalizing to new opponent behaviours, even when those opponents were trained under identical conditions." This is *precisely your symptom*: seed-to-seed win-rate variance is the macroscopic signature of JPC. Their fix is PSRO (populations + empirical-game meta-solver).
- Balduzzi et al., "Open-Ended Learning in Symmetric Zero-Sum Games" (ICML 2019): games have a transitive component and a cyclic (rock-paper-scissors) component; self-play climbs the transitive dimension but spins in the cyclic one; you need a population to cover cycles.
- Czarnecki et al., "Real World Games Look Like Spinning Tops" (NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2004.09468): in real games the non-transitive/cyclic dimension is largest at intermediate skill and shrinks near the top ("as we travel across the transitive dimension, the non-transitivity first [grows then shrinks]"). Implication for you: at the mediocre skill your 50M-step CPU budget reaches, you are likely in the *widest* part of the top — maximal cycling — which is the worst place to rely on a last iterate.
- Bansal et al., "Emergent Complexity via Multi-Agent Competition" (ICLR 2018): PPO self-play needs sampling opponents from *past checkpoints* to avoid overfitting to the current opponent.
- OpenAI Five (OpenAI, "Dota 2 with Large Scale Deep Reinforcement Learning," 2019, arXiv:1912.06680): pure PPO self-play worked at Dota 2 (simultaneous-move, imperfect-information, mild chance) — but explicitly with a dynamic opponent-sampling system (**80% current policy / 20% past selves**) "to maintain robustness and prevent strategy collapse," and at massive scale: it "experienced about 45,000 years of Dota self-play over ten real-time months … 770±50 PFlops/s·days of compute," then "defeated the Dota 2 world champions in a best-of-three match [OG, 2-0, April 13 2019] and 99.4% of human players." Existence proof that PPO self-play *can* work in your game class, but not from a naive single-opponent loop and not at CPU scale.
- AlphaStar (Vinyals et al., Nature 2019, s41586-019-1724-z): the entire motivation for **league training** (main agents + main/league exploiters, prioritized fictitious self-play) was that naive self-play chases cycles and forgets in StarCraft II — itself a simultaneous-move imperfect-information game. Verbatim: "across the full payoff, there are around 3,000,000 rock–paper–scissor cycles (with requirement of at least 70% win rates to form a cycle) that involve at least one exploiter," versus ~200 among main agents; main agents were made transitive by construction. AlphaStar "reached Grandmaster level, ranking among the top 0.2% of human players" (above 99.8% on Battle.net).
- Diplomacy / DORA (Bakhtin et al., "No-Press Diplomacy from Scratch," NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2110.02924): pure self-play from scratch (with equilibrium search + double-oracle action exploration) reached **superhuman in the 2-player zero-sum variant** and was unexploitable by experts — but the resulting equilibrium was "weird": DORA plays a strategy *incompatible* with human-bootstrapped agents, "presenting the first strong evidence of multiple equilibria in Diplomacy and suggest[ing] that self play alone may be insufficient." Later work (piKL / human-regularized RL, Jacob et al. 2022) added human anchoring. Relevance: even when from-scratch self-play converges to *an* equilibrium, in a game with many equilibria it may be one that is strong in self-play and strange against the field — which for you is the human ladder.

**Bottom line for §1:** your recipe can cycle between mutually-exploitable policies, and because both sides share one network and the game is near-symmetric, the win rate against *itself* stays ~0.5 by construction the entire time — the metric is structurally blind to the failure. You must measure convergence with something other than self-play win rate.

## 2. Detection using your existing artifacts + the noise-floor / evaluation methodology

You already save checkpoints and can run head-to-head matches. That is enough to diagnose cycling directly. Order these by cost.

**2.1 Cheapest cycle indicators (behavioral, no extra training).**
- **KL between consecutive checkpoints** and **aggregate behavioral statistics** (switch rate, average turns per battle, move-type distribution) as a time series. Monotone-then-flat = convergence-like; sustained oscillation = cycling. This is nearly free and is the first thing to plot.
- **Uniform-mixture-beats-last-iterate test.** Freeze the last K checkpoints, form the uniform mixture policy (sample a checkpoint per game), and play it against the single last iterate. **If the mixture beats the last iterate, that is the fingerprint of cycling** — it is exactly the gap that fictitious play / NFSP close by playing the time-average. This directly tests the §1.1 last-iterate-vs-time-average distinction with artifacts you already have.

**2.2 Within-run checkpoint round-robin (progress / forgetting).** Build the N×N win-rate matrix over your saved checkpoints from a single run. Two predictions: (i) *progress/transitivity* — later checkpoints should beat earlier ones (as in AlphaStar's main agents, which "behave transitively"); (ii) *forgetting* — if an *old* checkpoint beats a *new* one, you have catastrophic forgetting / cycling. Quantify the intransitivity: count the fraction of **cyclic triads** (i beats j, j beats k, k beats i) in the tournament, and/or do a **Hodge/Helmholtz decomposition** of the logit matrix into a transitive (gradient) component + a rotational (cyclic) component (the mathematics underlying Balduzzi 2019 and Czarnecki 2020). A large rotational component quantifies exactly how much of your "improvement" is going in circles.

**2.3 Cross-seed matrix (JPC).** Run the round-robin *across* your independently-seeded final policies. Lanctot et al. (2017) predict independent self-play will show high JPC: each seed's policy does well against itself/its own lineage and worse against foreign seeds. The average off-diagonal drop is a direct measure of overfitting-to-co-player and is the mechanistic explanation of your seed-to-seed variance. **This is documented in Pokémon specifically** — see §4 (Metamon's realistic-self-play model "believes it is playing" its own predecessor).

**2.4 Elo is the wrong summary; use Nash averaging.** When the payoff matrix has cycles, Elo/TrueSkill is misleading because it assumes transitivity — it will assign a single number to policies that are actually rock-paper-scissors. Use **Nash averaging** (Balduzzi, Tuyls, Perolat & Graepel, "Re-evaluating Evaluation," NeurIPS 2018, arXiv:1806.02643), which plays a meta-game on the evaluation matrix and "automatically adapts to redundancies in evaluation data … [and is] clone invariant," or the mElo2 extension that explicitly models a cyclic dimension. Report both a Nash-averaging rating and the cyclic-component magnitude.

**2.5 Approximate exploitability — the single most informative test.** Freeze a checkpoint and train a *best response* to it using your existing PPO code (fix the opponent, train a fresh policy to beat it). The gain of the BR over 0.5 is a lower bound on exploitability (Timbers et al., "Approximate Exploitability: Learning a Best Response in Large Games," IJCAI 2022, arXiv:2004.09677; their ISMCTS-BR is the search version, but a learned PPO BR is the model-free version and is what fits your setup — "freeze the policy, train a PPO best-responder against it … a learned BR underestimates the true best response, so this is a lower bound"). Interpretation: if a BR trained for a fraction of the original compute reaches, say, 70–80% against your "converged" agent, the agent is badly exploitable and the self-play win rate was lying. A *large* BR gain that is easy to find = you are nowhere near equilibrium. Cost: one extra PPO run per checkpoint tested, using far fewer steps than the main run (the opponent is fixed and stationary — a much easier problem). This is the test most directly tied to the theory in §1 and is worth building first among the training-based diagnostics.

**2.6 The noise floor — and why it is mostly a *training-seed* problem, not an eval problem.**
Decompose your ~0.10 MDE into two variance sources:
- **Evaluation (binomial) noise**, given a fixed pair of frozen policies. A single win rate over *n* games at p≈0.5 has SE = 0.5/√n. So n=100 → SE 0.05 (a 0.10 gap is ~2 SE, marginal); n=400 → SE 0.025; n=1000 → SE ≈0.016. To *resolve a 0.02–0.05 effect* between two fixed policies with unpaired evaluation you need on the order of n≈2·p(1−p)/SE² per arm — roughly 2,000 games per arm for SE≈0.011 (so a 0.05 gap ≈ 4.5 SE), and ~10,000+ per arm to cleanly see 0.02. This is affordable per-comparison.
- **Training-seed noise**, the variance of the *final policy itself* across independent training runs. This is *not* reduced by playing more evaluation games — it is a between-run component. Your report that final win rate varies seed-to-seed by ~0.10 means this component alone is ≈0.10, and it dominates. **No amount of extra evaluation games will lower a training-seed-driven MDE.** The only levers are: (a) more seeds (MDE on the mean shrinks as ~1/√(#seeds)); (b) variance reduction via paired evaluation; (c) reducing the underlying policy variance, which is what the algorithmic changes in §3 are *for*. This is the core reason your instrument can't see 0.02–0.05 levers — and it reframes the whole project: candidate levers should be judged partly on whether they *reduce seed variance*, not only whether they raise the mean.

**2.7 Variance reduction you can implement now (methodology, not tuning).**
- **Common random numbers / seed-locked paired evaluation.** Pokémon Showdown's battle stream accepts an explicit PRNG seed (and pkmn/engine exposes a `seed: [1,2,3,4]` argument directly). Evaluate A vs B and A′ vs B on the *same* seeds so damage rolls, crits, and status procs are shared; compare paired outcomes. Variance of the *difference* falls by the correlation between arms — often a large reduction in a high-chance game like Gen 1.
- **Team-swap / mirror matches.** Because random battles deal each side a random team, make both agents play *both* sides of the same team draw (swap and replay with the same RNG). This cancels the enormous team-draw variance, which in Gen 1 is a dominant nuisance term.
- **Report distributions, not points.** Follow Henderson et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning That Matters" (AAAI 2018, arXiv:1709.06560) — which documented exactly this seed-variance pathology in deep RL — and Agarwal et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" (NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2108.13264, the **rliable** library): report interquartile mean and stratified bootstrap confidence intervals across seeds, and use their performance-profile / probability-of-improvement metrics instead of a single mean win rate. This is the discipline that turns a ~0.10 MDE into something interpretable.

**2.8 The skill-vs-luck ceiling bounds every effect size you can ever see.** Per the Metamon paper (Grigsby et al., §2), even the very best human Gen 1 players have a GXE (probability of beating a random ladder opponent) of only **74–90%**: "Pokémon has the kind of inherent variance that would be familiar to Heads-Up No-Limit Texas Hold'em players: minimizing risk is considered a key skill, but some losses are inevitable. The very best players have a GXE between 74-90%." Gen 1/2 are "infamously stochastic." This is a hard cap: against the field, the entire dynamic range from random to world-best is roughly 0.5→~0.85 win rate, so a "large" lever might be worth only a few points of win rate *in absolute terms* even if it is a big relative improvement. Combined with §2.6, this is why your MDE must be pushed well below 0.10 by paired evaluation and multi-seed reporting, not by hoping for big effects.

## 3. Alternatives, ranked by evidence and cost

Axis legend for "games demonstrated": **SIM** = simultaneous moves, **HID** = hidden information, **CH** = chance. Pokémon Gen 1 RB = SIM + HID + heavy CH. Ranked from lowest-cost/best-fit to highest-cost/worst-fit for your constraints.

### 3.1 Past-checkpoint opponent pool / prioritized fictitious self-play (PFSP)
- **What:** sample opponents from a buffer of frozen past checkpoints (optionally prioritized by how much they beat the current agent), instead of always the current self.
- **Demonstrated in:** Dota 2 (OpenAI Five: SIM+HID+CH-mild) — *demonstrated with these properties*; StarCraft II (AlphaStar PFSP: SIM+HID+CH-mild) — *demonstrated with these properties*; Bansal 2018 sumo/MuJoCo (SIM, no HID) — assumed to transfer.
- **What it beat:** OpenAI Five beat Dota 2 world champions OG 2-0 (April 2019) and 99.4% of human players; AlphaStar reached Grandmaster / top-0.2% (above 99.8%) of the Battle.net ladder.
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no (only reset-to-start, which you have).
- **Integration cost:** small — tens of LOC (a checkpoint buffer + sampling), negligible compute multiplier (you already generate the games), no new algorithm. New failure mode: buffer management / storage; a stale buffer slows learning.
- **Evidence grade for your game class: moderate-to-strong.** This is the single best-evidenced change that respects the purity constraint (past *selves* are not human data or scripted opponents).

### 3.2 Magnetic Mirror Descent (MMD)-style proximal/KL-to-reference term on top of PPO
- **What:** add a KL(π‖π_ref) penalty to the PPO loss where π_ref is a periodically-refreshed snapshot of the policy; this is the term that provably converts recurrence into last-iterate QRE convergence (§1.2).
- **Demonstrated in:** 3×3 Dark Hex, Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe (alternating/HID/no-CH) as deep self-play; tabular normal- and extensive-form games (Kuhn/Leduc poker: alternating+HID+CH) — QRE convergence proven. **Not demonstrated in a heavy-chance simultaneous-move game.** So: *assumed to transfer* to Pokémon.
- **What it beat:** "competitive with CFR in tabular settings … competitive with those of PPO" (single-agent Atari/MuJoCo).
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no.
- **Integration cost:** very small — "a few lines on top of PPO" (a KL term against a reference network + a refresh schedule). Compute multiplier ≈1.0–1.1 (one extra forward pass for π_ref). New failure modes: the reference-refresh cadence is a new structural knob, and mis-set it degrades to plain PPO.
- **Evidence grade for your game class: moderate (theory) / weak (empirical in heavy chance).** But because it is nearly free and is the *theoretically correct* fix for §1, it is the highest expected-value algorithmic change.

### 3.3 Asymmetric / centralized critic that sees the opponent's hidden team during training only
- **What:** give the value network the opponent's hidden team/HP at training time (available in self-play since you control both sides), while the policy sees only the masked observation. Reduces value-estimation variance from hidden information.
- **Demonstrated in:** StarCraft II (AlphaStar's value net used opponent information during training: SIM+HID+CH) — *demonstrated with these properties*; Baisero & Amato, "Asymmetric Actor-Critic for POMDPs" (single-agent POMDP) — assumed to transfer.
- **What it beat:** component of AlphaStar; no standalone headline.
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no (you already have both teams in self-play).
- **Integration cost:** small-to-moderate — an extra input head on the critic + plumbing the hidden state through your DeepSets encoder; tens of LOC. Compute multiplier ≈1.0. New failure mode: train/test value mismatch (the critic is unavailable at deployment, so only the policy ships — fine, but advantage estimates must remain consistent).
- **Evidence grade: moderate.** Well-matched to your exact hidden-information structure; cheap; variance-reducing (helps §2.6). Does *not* by itself fix cycling.

### 3.4 Neural Replicator Dynamics (NeuRD) / regret-based policy gradients
- **What:** NeuRD (Hennes et al., AAMAS 2020, arXiv:1906.00190) replaces the softmax-PG update with a replicator/regret-matching-consistent update; Srinivasan et al., "Actor-Critic Policy Optimization in Partially Observable Multiagent Environments" (NeurIPS 2018) connect actor-critic to regret minimization in IIEFGs.
- **Demonstrated in:** Kuhn/Leduc poker, small IIEFGs (alternating+HID+CH) — assumed to transfer to SIM+heavy-CH.
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no.
- **Integration cost:** moderate — the loss changes (a few dozen LOC), but it is a genuine change to the update rule and its interaction with GAE/clipping is not standard. Compute ≈1.0.
- **Evidence grade: weak** for your class; R-NaD/MMD subsume the practically-useful part with more evidence.

### 3.5 R-NaD (full DeepNash recipe)
- **What:** reward transformation with log-ratio regularization terms, NeuRD loss, v-trace, and periodic regularization-policy resets (Perolat et al. 2021/2022).
- **Demonstrated in:** **Stratego** (alternating, HID, **no chance**) — DeepNash reached top-3 human expert level on Gravon; also Liar's Poker (alternating+HID+CH) via OpenSpiel R-NaD (Sokota-team-adjacent, arXiv:2511.03724). So the *chance* axis is covered by Liar's Poker but the flagship result is a **no-chance, alternating** game. *Assumed to transfer* to SIM+heavy-CH.
- **What it beat:** beat the strongest existing Stratego bots ("winning 97.1 percent of 800 games") and human experts on Gravon, achieving a rating of 1799 and **3rd place** in 2022 (top two ratings were 1868 and 1831); 42/50 (84%) of expert matches won.
- **Search at inference:** no (DeepNash is search-free). **Cloneable simulator:** no.
- **Integration cost:** large — a substantially different training loop (reward transform + NeuRD + v-trace + reset schedule), not a patch on PPO; expect a multi-week rewrite and several new interacting components/failure modes. Compute multiplier moderate.
- **Evidence grade: moderate in principle, weak as a cost-adjusted choice for you.** MMD gives ~80% of the theoretical benefit for ~5% of the engineering cost. Prefer MMD unless MMD demonstrably fails your exploitability test.

### 3.6 PSRO / Pipeline-PSRO / NeuPL / league training
- **What:** maintain a population; iteratively train best responses to a meta-strategy (Nash/uniform) over the population; leagues add main/exploiter roles (Lanctot 2017; McAleer et al. Pipeline-PSRO 2020; Liu et al. NeuPL 2021; AlphaStar).
- **Demonstrated in:** Leduc poker, gridworlds (Lanctot 2017); StarCraft II (AlphaStar league: SIM+HID+CH) — *demonstrated with these properties*.
- **What it beat:** AlphaStar → Grandmaster; PSRO → lower JPC / approximate Nash in Leduc, and "converge to close to an approximate Nash Equilibrium much faster than Neural Fictitious Self-Play."
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no, but needs to train many best responses.
- **Integration cost:** large — a population manager, meta-solver, and N× the training runs. Compute multiplier ≈ population size (prohibitive at 50M steps CPU-only for a full league; a *minimal* 2–3 agent version is closer to §3.1).
- **Evidence grade: strong that it fixes cycling; weak on feasibility for you.** The principled cure for intransitivity, but the compute multiplier is the wrong shape for a single CPU-only developer. A degenerate one-main-plus-one-exploiter version overlaps with §3.1/§3.3.

### 3.7 Neural Fictitious Self-Play (NFSP)
- **What:** maintain a DQN best-responder + a supervised average-policy network trained on a reservoir buffer, with anticipatory mixing (Heinrich & Silver, arXiv:1603.01121, 2016).
- **Demonstrated in:** Leduc and limit Texas Hold'em (alternating+HID+CH) — assumed to transfer to SIM.
- **What it beat:** approached Nash in Leduc; competitive in limit hold'em; later beaten by Deep CFR (NFSP converged to 47 mbb/g exploitability where Deep CFR reached 37 mbb/g in flop hold'em).
- **Search at inference:** no. **Cloneable simulator:** no.
- **Integration cost:** large and *architecturally mismatched* — NFSP was designed around a **DQN** best-responder plus a separate average-policy network and reservoir buffer; grafting it onto a from-scratch PPO actor-critic is a rewrite, not an add-on. Compute multiplier ~2× (two networks).
- **Evidence grade: weak** for your class and poor cost fit. Note: the *average-policy* idea is capturable far more cheaply by §2.1's checkpoint-mixture (essentially a poor man's fictitious play for *evaluation*).

### 3.8 Deep CFR and successors (DREAM, ARMAC, ESCHER)
- **What:** neural counterfactual regret minimization (Brown et al., ICML 2019, arXiv:1811.00164); DREAM/ESCHER remove the need for exact traversals / importance sampling.
- **Demonstrated in:** poker variants — flop hold'em (FHP), Leduc (alternating+HID+CH). "Deep CFR reaches an exploitability of 37 mbb/g while NFSP converges to 47 mbb/g." *Assumed to transfer* to SIM; note CFR's native formulation is for sequential (alternating) games — simultaneous moves must be encoded as an information-set construction.
- **Search at inference:** no (for the average strategy). **Cloneable simulator: YES for vanilla Deep CFR** — external-sampling traversals require branching from arbitrary states. DREAM/ESCHER relax this to model-free sampling.
- **Integration cost:** very large — a different algorithm entirely (regret networks, traversal/sampling machinery), not a modification of PPO. The traversal requirement collides with the Node.js Showdown simulator (poke-env) which is not designed for cheap arbitrary-state cloning.
- **Evidence grade: weak-to-none** as a practical choice for you: wrong paradigm, high cost, and its cloneable-simulator dependency is a poor match for the poke-env/Showdown stack (though **pkmn/engine**, see §3.10, would make it feasible in principle).

### 3.9 Search at inference (SM-MCTS / decoupled UCT / ReBeL / Student of Games)
- **What:** at decision time, run simultaneous-move MCTS with decoupled/regret-matching selection at each node (Shafiei et al. 2009; Lanctot et al. 2013 on Goofspiel; Tak, Lanctot & Winands 2014; Online Outcome Sampling — Lisý, Lanctot & Bowling, AAMAS 2015), or full search+RL (ReBeL, Brown et al. 2020; Student of Games, Schmid et al. 2023).
- **Demonstrated in:** Goofspiel (**SIM**+HID-variant+CH-in-deck-order) — *demonstrated with these properties, and the closest match in the classical literature to your axes*; poker (ReBeL: alternating+HID+CH); Student of Games spans chess/Go/poker/Scotland Yard.
- **What it beat:** SM-MCTS is standard in Goofspiel/Tron; ReBeL reached superhuman heads-up no-limit poker.
- **Search at inference: YES (that's the point).** **Cloneable simulator: YES** — MCTS needs to branch from the current state, requiring a fast cloneable engine.
- **Integration cost:** moderate-to-large, and it is *orthogonal to your training method* — it wraps your trained policy/value at deployment. This is what the strong Showdown bots actually do (§4): **Foul Play** runs SM search (formerly expectiminimax over joint-action payoff matrices, now MCTS with damage-roll grouping) over the Rust **poke-engine** with team prediction; **PokeChamp** runs depth-limited minimax with an LLM prior. Feasible on CPU *if* you use a fast cloneable engine.
- **Evidence grade: strong that search helps in Pokémon specifically** (Foul Play, PokeChamp, Wang 2024's PPO+MCTS all improve with it). **But it does not fix the self-play convergence question** — it is a deployment-time amplifier of whatever policy/value you learned, and it violates the spirit of "search-free" if that is part of your novelty claim.

### 3.10 Enabling infrastructure: fast cloneable Gen 1 engine
Not an algorithm but a feasibility gate for §3.8/§3.9. **pkmn/engine (libpkmn)** is a Zig implementation with C/TypeScript/WASM bindings, Gen 1 support, explicit `seed` control, and is advertised as **>1000× faster than Pokémon Showdown** in compatibility mode; Foul Play's **poke-engine** is a Rust engine that generates the full outcome distribution per joint action and supports state mutate/reverse for cheap search. The Node.js Showdown simulator that poke-env drives is *not* built for cheap arbitrary-state cloning. **Implication:** any path that needs branching/traversal (search, Deep CFR) is only CPU-feasible on libpkmn/poke-engine, not on poke-env. Even for pure PPO, libpkmn's throughput could materially change what 50M steps costs in wall-clock and how many seeds you can afford — which directly attacks the §2.6 seed-variance problem.

## 4. Pokémon-specific prior work

**The single most important finding: no published or open-sourced agent has reached a strong level in any Pokémon Showdown format via pure self-play from scratch.** Every strong agent used human data, inference-time search, team prediction, or an LLM prior.

- **Lee & Togelius (2017), "Showdown AI Competition":** proposed Competitive Pokémon Singles / Showdown as an AI benchmark; established the competition and heuristic baselines. Foundational, not an RL result.
- **Kalose, Kaya & Kim (2018):** DQN for Gen 1; academic-course-level, weak play; used the Showdown simulator.
- **Huang & Lee (2019), "A Self-Play Policy Optimization Approach to Battling Pokémon" (IEEE CoG):** **PPO self-play without tree search** — the closest prior work to your setup. Per Metamon's Appendix A.2, they "achieve a 1677 Glicko-1 and 72% GXE on the Gen7RandomBattle Pokémon Showdown ladder." Note this is Gen 7, reaches only intermediate GXE, and is not documented as strictly pure-from-scratch with no shaping/curriculum; treat "72% GXE via PPO self-play" as the empirical anchor for what self-play-style PPO has achieved in a random-battle format.
- **Wang (2024):** augments PPO with **test-time MCTS**, reaching "a 1756 Glicko-1 and 79.5% GXE on the Gen4RandomBattle ladder" (per Metamon Appendix A.2). Search at inference materially helped.
- **Metamon / Grigsby et al. (2025), "Human-Level Competitive Pokémon via Scalable Offline RL with Transformers" (RLC 2025, arXiv:2504.04395):** offline RL on 5M+ reconstructed *human* replays (Gen 1–4), Transformers up to 200M params, **search-free**. Critical facts for you:
  - **They did not run pure self-play from scratch.** All their self-play data is *added on top of* the human dataset; "from scratch" in their §5.3 means fresh weights on the *human+synthetic* mixture, never self-play alone with no human data. Their Conclusion explicitly leaves it as *future* work: "Alternative training details and large-scale self-play techniques may create a path to super-human performance" (speculative — "may").
  - Their human-data offline-RL policy (Large-RL, 200M) is "officially estimated to have a 41-58% chance to defeat a randomly sampled opponent (depending on the generation)."
  - Adding self-play/synthetic data on top raised this: "The resulting agents improve to win rates of 64-80% — rising into the top 10% of active usernames and onto the global leaderboards." Their best model, **SynRL-V2**, reached **Gen1OU GXE 79.9% (Glicko-1 1761 ± 35)** over 613 human battles, peak global rank **#31 in Gen1OU** — "the first time an AI has achieved any of SynRL-V2's ladder ratings in any of the Early-Gen OU tiers."
  - A cautionary self-play finding directly relevant to §2.3: their *realistic* self-play model became "significantly better against itself … but this will translate to inconsistent improvement against real players … Battle replays make it clear that the model believes it is playing SynRL-V1." **This is documented overfitting-to-co-player (JPC) in Pokémon specifically.**
  - They held/beat Foul Play ("play the best version of the bot to a draw in Gens 3 and 4 … and outperform it in the long horizons of Gens 1 and 2") and dominated PokéLLMon (e.g., 74-1 out of 75 in Gen 1). They did **not** directly evaluate PokéChamp in early gens (concurrent work; engine incompatibility).
  - Human ceiling (their §2): best humans have **GXE 74–90%**; "Gen1 and Gen2 are infamously stochastic."
- **PokéChamp (Karten, Nguyen & Jin, ICML 2025, arXiv:2503.04094):** depth-limited **minimax with an LLM prior** (LLM does action sampling, opponent modeling, value estimation), no additional LLM training — Gen 9 OU. With GPT-4o it "achieves a win rate of 76% against the best existing LLM-based bot and 84% against the strongest rule-based bot"; projected **Elo 1300–1500, top 30–10%** of human players. Search + human-data-derived priors, not self-play.
- **PokéLLMon (Hu et al., 2024):** first LLM agent for Showdown; human-parity in some Gen 8/9 settings but "uncompetitive in the long horizons of the early generations" (per Metamon §5.4).
- **Foul Play (pmariglia):** search-based bot; **poke-engine** (Rust) with root-parallelized MCTS + damage-roll grouping and team prediction; former expectiminimax over joint-action payoff matrices (dropped because "the opponent's move being selected after the bot's move … is not how Pokémon works: both players select their moves at the same time"). Ladder-competitive across generations (reached ~#4 on the Gen 3 OU ladder in the author's testing). Was a **co-winning entry in the NeurIPS 2025 PokéAgent battling track**.
- **NeurIPS 2025 PokéAgent Challenge (Karten et al., arXiv:2603.15563):** 100+ teams / 650+ people, 15k+ battles. **Battling-track winners: PA-Agent (RL) and Foul Play (MCTS); Judges' Choice: Porygon2 (League), August (LLM).** Organizers' summary: "RL specialists dominate in partially observable 2p0s games while hybrid RL-LLM solutions are best for high-stakes long-context tasks." Foul Play's winning entry used "Root-Parallelized MCTS with a custom Rust-based engine … [and] Damage Roll Grouping." Metamon served as "both a starter kit and winning baseline." **No pure-from-scratch-self-play entry appears among the winners.**
- **VGC AI (Reis et al.)** and **poke-env (Sahovic 2020)** baselines: poke-env is the standard Python interface (drives the Node.js Showdown sim) and is now the default harness (Metamon uses a customized fork). Game-theoretic/Nash analyses of Pokémon exist mostly at the single-turn matrix level (the "Nash-Equilibrium" battle-bot mode in the earlier Showdown bot lineage), not full-game equilibrium solving.

**Verdict for §4:** the evidence is consistent and one-directional. Self-play-style PPO (Huang & Lee) tops out around 72% GXE in a *later* generation; the state of the art in early-gen (Metamon, ~80% Gen 1 GXE) needed human replays; the strongest general bots (Foul Play, PokeChamp, PokéAgent winners) need inference-time search and/or team prediction and/or LLM priors. Your pure-from-scratch self-play project would be genuinely novel *because it has not been shown to work* — which is exactly why the convergence and detection questions in §§1–2 are the crux.

## 5. The big levers, ranked by expected effect size vs. your ~0.10 detection threshold

For each: expected effect direction, whether it is likely *visible* to your current instrument, and evidence grade.

1. **Fix the instrument first (paired/CRN evaluation + team-swap + multi-seed rliable reporting).** *Not a lever on the agent — a lever on your ability to see levers.* Expected to cut your MDE from ~0.10 to well under 0.05 by removing team-draw and RNG variance and by averaging over seeds. **Visibility: this is what creates visibility.** Evidence: strong (Henderson 2018; Agarwal 2021). **Do this before anything else**, or you cannot evaluate items 2–6.
2. **Past-checkpoint opponent pool / PFSP.** Expected to reduce cycling and seed variance (the mechanism behind your symptom) and modestly raise the mean. **Visibility: likely visible** — it attacks the variance component directly, and OpenAI Five/AlphaStar treat it as essential, not marginal. Evidence: moderate-strong.
3. **MMD KL-to-reference proximal term.** Expected to give last-iterate convergence toward a QRE, reducing exploitability and seed variance. **Visibility: visible on the exploitability/cross-seed diagnostics (§2), possibly marginal on raw win rate.** Evidence: moderate (theory) / weak (heavy-chance empirics). Near-zero cost, so high expected value.
4. **Inference-time SM-MCTS (if you relax the search-free claim).** Expected effect on win rate is *large* by Pokémon-specific evidence (Foul Play, PokeChamp, Wang 2024). **Visibility: almost certainly visible even at MDE 0.10.** Evidence: strong for Pokémon. Caveat: orthogonal to your training-purity thesis and needs a fast cloneable engine.
5. **Asymmetric critic (opponent team visible to value net in training).** Expected to reduce advantage-estimate variance → faster, more stable learning, lower seed variance. **Visibility: possibly visible via reduced seed variance; small effect on the mean.** Evidence: moderate.
6. **R-NaD / PSRO-league / NFSP / Deep CFR.** Expected effect uncertain and likely modest-relative-to-cost in your class; each introduces large engineering and (for leagues) compute multipliers. **Visibility: R-NaD's effect may be *below* your current MDE and only detectable via exploitability; leagues would be visible but are compute-infeasible at your budget.** Evidence: weak-to-none (cost-adjusted) for your specific class.

**Key cross-cutting point:** because the human skill-vs-luck ceiling in Gen 1 is only ~74–90% GXE (§2.8), and because most of your uncertainty is *training-seed* variance (§2.6), the levers most worth chasing are those that **reduce policy variance across seeds** (items 1–3, 5), not only those that raise a single-run mean. Your current instrument is blind to exactly the levers that matter most, which is why item 1 is non-negotiable and comes first.

## 6. Bottom-line recommendation

**Keep PPO. Do not switch algorithms. Augment, in this order:**

1. **Rebuild evaluation before touching the agent.** Implement seed-locked common-random-number evaluation using Showdown's explicit PRNG seed; make both agents play both sides of each team draw; report IQM + bootstrap CIs across ≥5–10 training seeds with rliable. Add the cheap cycle diagnostics (§2.1) and the within-run + cross-seed checkpoint round-robins with cyclic-triad / Hodge decomposition and Nash averaging (§2.2–2.4). Build the approximate-exploitability best-response harness from your existing PPO code (§2.5). *Rationale:* you cannot distinguish good ideas from bad ones until the MDE is below the effect sizes you care about, and this is pure methodology — no hyperparameter tuning.
2. **Add a past-checkpoint opponent pool (PFSP-lite).** Lowest-cost agent change with the best evidence in your game class; directly targets the JPC/cycling that produces your seed variance. Consistent with purity (past selves only).
3. **Add the MMD-style KL-to-reference term.** A few lines; it is the theoretically correct fix for the Poincaré-recurrence problem and the only change that converts your dynamics into ones with a last-iterate convergence guarantee (to a QRE). Verify its effect on *exploitability*, not just win rate.
4. **Consider an asymmetric critic** if seed variance is still limiting after 2–3.
5. **Only if you relax the search-free constraint**, add inference-time SM-MCTS on a fast cloneable engine (libpkmn/poke-engine) — by Pokémon-specific evidence this is the largest single win-rate lever, but it is a deployment amplifier, not a fix to self-play convergence, and it changes your novelty claim.

**Do not** adopt R-NaD, NFSP, Deep CFR, or a full league as a first move: cost-adjusted evidence for your specific class (SIM + HID + heavy CH + CPU-only) is weak, and MMD + checkpoint pool capture most of the benefit for a tiny fraction of the effort.

**Benchmarks that would change this recommendation:**
- If, after item 1, your cross-seed matrix shows **low JPC and small cyclic component** and approximate-exploitability best responses gain **< ~0.10** over your agent → your recipe is closer to equilibrium than feared; stop adding machinery and just scale seeds/steps.
- If exploitability best responses gain **> ~0.30** and the uniform-checkpoint-mixture beats the last iterate → cycling is severe; escalate from item 2 to item 3, and if item 3 fails the same test, R-NaD becomes justified despite its cost.
- If win rate against a fixed strong external reference (e.g., Foul Play or a Metamon checkpoint) is **flat across 50M steps** regardless of items 2–3 → the bottleneck is representation/credit-assignment, not equilibrium dynamics, and inference-time search (item 5) is the pragmatic answer.

## 7. What is genuinely unknown / where the literature is silent

- **No convergence theory covers your exact class.** IIEFG + chance + neural function approximation has no last-iterate guarantee for any policy-gradient method, regularized or not. R-NaD/MMD proofs are tabular/first-order; DeepNash is empirical and in a *no-chance* game. Whether the QRE-convergence story survives heavy stochasticity + function approximation is open.
- **No demonstration of pure from-scratch self-play in any Showdown format.** Huang & Lee (PPO self-play, 72% GXE Gen 7) is the closest, and it is neither documented as strictly pure-from-scratch nor in Gen 1. Whether 50M CPU steps is even in the right order of magnitude is unknown — OpenAI Five needed ~45,000 simulated years and 770±50 PFlops/s·days; DeepNash and AlphaStar used far larger budgets.
- **Effect of heavy chance on cycling is under-studied.** The spinning-top/JPC literature is mostly low-or-no-chance games. Whether Gen 1's stochasticity *smooths* the payoff landscape (reducing intransitivity) or *masks* cycles behind noise (making them harder to detect) is not established — a genuine research question your diagnostics could answer.
- **Multiple equilibria.** DORA showed from-scratch self-play can find an equilibrium that is strong in self-play but strange against humans, giving "the first strong evidence of multiple equilibria." Whether Gen 1 random battles has a benign near-unique equilibrium or DORA-like multiplicity is unknown, and it bears directly on whether a purely self-play agent will transfer to the human ladder at all.
- **The exact skill-vs-luck ceiling in Gen 1 *random* battles** (as opposed to Gen 1 OU) is not cleanly reported anywhere located; the 74–90% human GXE figure is Metamon's cross-generation number. This bounds your maximum observable effect sizes but is itself imprecise for your specific format.
